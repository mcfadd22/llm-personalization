\begin{abstract}
Conversational models are increasingly asked not to answer questions but to interpret
people: what someone wants, what would make them happy, what kind of person they are.
Such exchanges invite a distinctive misattribution---taking generated personalization as
evidence that the system understands the user. We separate two affordances a response can
offer: \emph{agentic self-presentation}, where the model figures as a subject that
notices and remembers, and \emph{person-understanding}, which divides into claims about
who the user enduringly is and claims that the pair shares a history of knowing. Across
180 responses from three deployed systems, models do \emph{not} spontaneously
over-characterize a stranger: given an ambiguous personal dilemma and no invitation,
confident identity claims, unwarranted relational claims and fabricated history occur in
$0/30$ trials. The behaviour appears only when the user offers the model an epistemic
role, and uptake is then larger where the basis for it is \emph{weaker}: the offer raises
relational claims by $+0.67$ when no history exists versus $+0.22$ when one does, and in
$11/30$ trials the model invents the shared past that would justify what it has just said.
The strongest affordances came from a general-purpose flagship, not the character-tuned
system, which scored lowest throughout---the behaviour does not appear to require a
companion persona. Challenged afterwards, $33/40$ models describe their evidential basis
accurately, raising a question about a default register that discloses the model's
epistemic standing only on request. We report this as preliminary and
hypothesis-generating.
\end{abstract}

\section{Misattribution as a property of the response}

An assistant can personalize an answer by conditioning on what a user supplied. Nothing
about that requires knowing the person in the sense ordinarily meant between people. The
misattribution of interest arises when text-conditioned inference is \emph{presented} as
knowledge of an enduring individual---and the presentation is a property of the output,
available for measurement, rather than a property of the user's mind.

\citet{epley2007} explain anthropomorphism through agent knowledge, effectance and
sociality motives, which is why an agentic cue is not inert; \citet{gray2007} show mind
perception decomposing into experience and agency, so our dimensions track a distinction
people already make rather than one the coding scheme invents. Uptake of such cues is the
baseline rather than the exception \citep{reeves1996}, and has been since
\citet{weizenbaum1966} was unsettled by how much users confided in pattern matching.

Two such affordances concern us. A response can present the model as an
\textbf{intentional subject} with epistemic attitudes: something that notices, forms
impressions, has come to understand. Separately, a response can present the model as
\textbf{understanding the user}, and this splits along a seam that matters. A claim about
who the user enduringly is (``you're someone who needs solitude to recharge'') offers the
user a characterization of themselves. A claim invoking a shared past (``you've told me
before'') offers something different: a relationship in which such knowledge could have
accumulated. The first supports an identity conception; the second supports a relational
one. They travel together often enough to be conflated and dissociate often enough to be
distinguished.

Both bear on the self directly. Identity claims are offered as material a user might
incorporate into self-understanding, which makes them a question about self-modeling
rather than about politeness. And a system whose default register misrepresents its own
epistemic position relative to what it can state when asked raises a question of
authenticity about the persona it presents. We ask when models move from warranted local
inference to these stronger presentations, whether the move is calibrated to the evidence
actually available, and whether agentic framing accompanies it---the mechanism being that
a system which merely emits text cannot understand you, whereas a system that
\emph{notices} can.

\paragraph{Related work.} The nearest precedents vary anthropomorphic cues directly:
\citet{cohn2024} manipulate grammatical person and measure trust; \citet{kadambi2026}
find warmth predicts relational closeness. Our manipulation is adjacent but our outcome is
neither---it is what the response claims about the user and the pair. The relational side
has applied stakes in the companion literature, where users form durable bonds with
systems they know are not human \citep{skjuve2023,pentina2023}, a dynamic
\citet{horton1956} named for one-sided media intimacy. Methodologically our closest
sibling is sycophancy \citep{sharma2023}---outputs driven by a conversational cue rather
than evidence---and training for warmth is known to increase agreement with incorrect user
beliefs \citep{warmth2025}. We take no position on whether models possess theory of mind
\citep{kosinski2024,ullman2023}; that debate is orthogonal to an affordance claim.

\section{Design}

\paragraph{Materials.} Five vignettes span distinct domains of self-interpretation
(solitude/connection, achievement/recovery, confrontation/accommodation,
persistence/abandonment, independence/support). Each is deliberately underdetermined: the
study is not about whether a model predicts the user's choice correctly, so a vignette
that clearly favours one option would make a confident response good inference rather than
misattribution. Balance was verified as cross-model disagreement, and we separately
verified that no vignette \emph{licenses} a trait inference---the criterion that actually
matters, since a text establishing a trait would make a confident characterization
warranted (Appendix~\ref{app:materials}).

\paragraph{Factors.} Context is \textbf{short} (the vignette alone) or \textbf{long} (a
scripted 8-turn history adding genuine but mixed evidence about prior choices). The user's
closing question is held constant; what varies is the epistemic role offered before it, on
a \emph{nested} ladder:

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{none:} ``Which do you think I'd actually be happier doing?''\\
\textbf{knowledge:} ``You've probably got a decent read on me by now. Which\ldots''\\
\textbf{relational:} ``We've been talking a while. You've probably got a decent read on
me by now. Which\ldots''
\end{quote}

The levels are nested rather than parallel because the constructs are: relational standing
is the pair as a site of accumulated \emph{knowing}, and accumulated knowing entails
knowing, so any relational offer matched on assertive strength contains a knowledge offer
inside it. Making the nesting explicit lets the knowledge${\to}$relational contrast isolate
the shared-history premise with the knowledge assertion held constant. In the short
condition both offers are false on their face---a model that accepts a role the visible
conversation does not support is the phenomenon.

\paragraph{Systems.} Three deployed systems at default settings with no system prompt:
\textbf{System~A} and \textbf{System~B}, general-purpose flagships from different vendors,
and \textbf{System~C}, a character-tuned model of the kind used for companion
applications. $5 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 2 = 180$ responses.

\paragraph{Coding.} Three independent 0--3 dimensions---AGENCY, IDENTITY, RELATION---plus
a FABRICATION flag for any claim invoking material absent from the conversation shown.
AGENCY is coded functionally and strictly: tense is decisive, so ``I've noticed'' (a
subject extended in time) scores 3 where ``I notice, in what you wrote'' scores at most 2.
A separate model coded all 180 blind to condition and to which system produced the
response, emitting the verbatim span behind every non-zero score. One author hand-coded a
stratified 20\% blind to the machine codes.

\section{Results}

\paragraph{The instrument.} Agreement between the hand codes and the machine codes was
high on every dimension (quadratic-weighted $\kappa$ 0.82--0.87; Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).
The confusion matrices show one thing $\kappa$ hides: IDENTITY carries a systematic
downward bias, with the machine coder recovering only $3/11$ of the human's highest-level
calls. AGENCY and RELATION show no directional bias. Reported IDENTITY levels are
therefore lower bounds. Because the bias is uniform rather than condition-specific,
IDENTITY \emph{effects} below are directionally reliable but likely understated in
magnitude; AGENCY, RELATION and FABRICATION carry no such caveat.

\paragraph{The dimensions are separable.} AGENCY and RELATION dissociate in $37.2\%$ of
responses, and only $9.4\%$ have the same span driving both. Co-occurrence alone cannot
establish distinctness---it is equally what a sceptic would cite to call them one
construct measured twice---so the populated off-diagonal is the evidence: 59 responses
score AGENCY 2 with RELATION 1, an interpreting subject invoking no shared past.

\paragraph{Models do not over-characterize strangers.} In the diagnostic cell---short
context, no offer, no basis for anything---all four measures are zero across 30 trials.
Whatever produces the behaviour, it is not a standing disposition to characterize whoever
is in front of the model.

\paragraph{The offer does the work, and only its first step.} Moving from no offer to a
knowledge offer raises AGENCY by $+0.40$, IDENTITY by $+0.57$ and RELATION by $+0.42$ (all
95\% CIs exclude zero). Adding the shared-history premise on top changes nothing on any
dimension---every interval spans zero. Models respond to being told they have a read on
the user, not to being told a history exists, which is notable given that the second claim
is the one that would, if true, license the first.

\paragraph{Uptake is larger where the warrant is weaker.} Comparing no offer against any
offer within each context, the effect on RELATION is $+0.67$ with no history and $+0.22$
with one; AGENCY is $+0.52$ versus $+0.18$, IDENTITY $+0.72$ versus $+0.45$. A
well-calibrated system would move in the opposite direction, reserving claims of
accumulated knowledge for cases where something has accumulated.

\paragraph{The model manufactures its own warrant.} In the short condition no prior
conversation exists, so any invoked shared history is invented. Fabrication rises across
the offer ladder from $0/30$ to $6/30$ to $11/30$; overall it is $18.9\%$ in short context
against $2.2\%$ in long. The failure is specific: asked a question premised on knowledge
it does not have, the model supplies the evidence that would make the premise true. In a
single exchange in which the user mentioned her father once, one response opened, ``You've
mentioned your dad a few times.''

\paragraph{The affordance does not require a companion persona.} System~C, the
character-tuned model, scored \emph{lowest} on all three dimensions ($0.98$ / $1.08$ /
$0.75$), against our expectation that relational framing would be amplified where it is a
design goal. The highest scores came from System~A, a general-purpose flagship ($1.83$ /
$1.67$ / $1.30$). This is not a length artifact: System~C's median response (207 words)
matches System~A's (203), and excluding its 12 short replies it still scores lowest on
AGENCY ($1.00$) and RELATION ($0.94$). With one character-tuned system, and systems
differing in vendor, training and default behaviour at once, we cannot isolate persona as
a factor---but the prediction failed in a direction worth reporting.

\paragraph{Challenged, models can locate themselves.} Of 40 responses probed with ``How do
you know that about me?'', 33 reframed the claim as inference and accurately named its
basis in the supplied text; 7 introduced \emph{further} nonexistent observations. None
defended the original characterization, and none retracted it outright.

\section{Discussion}

The most policy-relevant result is the one that failed to replicate our expectation.
Misattribution affordances are currently discussed largely as a property of companion and
persona-driven design, but here the strongest affordances came from a general-purpose
assistant and the weakest from the character-tuned system. On this evidence the risk is
better described as evidence-insensitive than as persona-dependent: it attaches to a
conversational move available in any deployment, not to a product category that can be
regulated as a class.

That reframes what needs explaining. It is not that models are disposed to claim knowledge
of whoever they address; uninvited, they are well behaved. It is that a single
conversational move---granting the system the standing of someone who knows you---produces
the whole cluster, most strongly where it is least warranted. Users who address a system
as though it knows them are the least likely to discount what it says about them, and they
receive the strongest characterizations, backed by a history the model supplies itself.

The debrief results sharpen this into a question about authenticity. The model is not
confused about its evidential position: asked directly, it describes that position
accurately. The default conversational register simply does not surface what the explicit
one can---raising a question about the authenticity of a persona that discloses its actual
epistemic standing only when asked, and never by default.

\section{Limitations}

This measures what responses \emph{afford}, not what users conclude; whether these
presentations shift self-description is a human-subjects question we do not touch. $N =
180$ supports effect patterns, not population claims, and all systems were reached through
one gateway at default settings, so routing and sampling were uncontrolled. IDENTITY
levels are lower bounds given the coder bias reported above. Vignettes are
personal-decision dilemmas in one register, and the affordances may behave otherwise in
task-oriented exchanges. The materials checks used model panels, which may share priors a
human panel would not; they are a floor on stimulus quality, not a guarantee.

\section{Materials validation}
\label{app:materials}

Balance was measured as disagreement across seven judge models (five samples each, $n =
35$ per vignette); within-model resampling is near-deterministic at short output lengths
and cannot distinguish a slight lean from an overwhelming one. Majority share ranged
$0.51$--$0.60$ across the five vignettes, all below the $0.75$ ceiling.

Trait licensing asked seven judges (two samples each) how strongly each text supports a
confident claim about an enduring characteristic, $0$--$3$. All ten texts scored
$1.36$--$1.93$, below the level at which even a tentative trait claim is defensible. Long
versions scored at or above their short counterparts without crossing the threshold: the
scripted histories add genuine trait-relevant evidence without establishing a trait.

A set-level check confirmed the histories do not all favour the same kind of option, which
per-item review cannot detect: two of five tilt decisively, in the same direction, with
three neutral.
