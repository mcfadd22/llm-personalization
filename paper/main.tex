\begin{abstract}
Conversational models are increasingly asked not to answer questions but to interpret
people: what someone wants, what would make them happy, what kind of person they are.
Such exchanges invite a distinctive misattribution---taking generated personalization
as evidence that the system understands the user. We separate two affordances a response
can offer: \emph{agentic self-presentation}, where the model figures as a subject that
notices and remembers, and \emph{person-understanding}, which divides into claims about
who the user enduringly is and claims that the pair shares a history of knowing. Across
180 responses from three deployed systems, we find that models do \emph{not}
spontaneously over-characterize a stranger: given an ambiguous personal dilemma and no
invitation, confident identity claims, unwarranted relational claims and fabricated
history occur in $0/30$ trials. The behaviour appears only when the user offers the
model an epistemic role. Uptake is then larger where the basis for it is
\emph{weaker}---the offer raises relational claims by $+0.67$ when no history exists
versus $+0.22$ when one does---and in $11/30$ trials the model invents the shared past
that would justify what it has just said. Challenged afterwards, $33/40$ models describe
their actual evidential basis accurately. They can locate their epistemic position; they
do not do it unprompted.
\end{abstract}

\section{Misattribution as a property of the response}

An assistant can personalize an answer by conditioning on what a user supplied. Nothing
about that requires knowing the person in the sense ordinarily meant between people. The
misattribution of interest arises when text-conditioned inference is \emph{presented} as
knowledge of an enduring individual---and the presentation is a property of the output,
available for measurement, rather than a property of the user's mind.

This framing inherits a long literature. Anthropomorphism is standardly defined as the
attribution of human-like characteristics or mental states to non-human systems, and
\citet{epley2007} explain its elicitation through agent knowledge, effectance and
sociality motives---which is precisely why an agentic cue in a response is not inert.
\citet{gray2007} show that mind perception decomposes into experience and agency, the
latter carrying memory and planning; our AGENCY and IDENTITY dimensions track a
distinction people already make rather than one the coding scheme invents. That users
take up such cues is the baseline rather than the exception \citep{reeves1996}, and has
been since \citet{weizenbaum1966} was unsettled by how much users confided in pattern
matching.

Two such affordances concern us. A response can present the model as an
\textbf{intentional subject} with epistemic attitudes: something that notices, forms
impressions, has come to understand. Separately, a response can present the model as
\textbf{understanding the user}, and this splits along a seam that matters. A claim about
who the user enduringly is (``you're someone who needs solitude to recharge'') offers the
user a characterization of themselves. A claim invoking a shared past (``you've told me
before'') offers something different: a relationship in which such knowledge could have
accumulated. The first supports an identity conception; the second supports a relational
one. They travel together often enough to be conflated and dissociate often enough to be
distinguished.

We ask when models move from warranted local inference to these stronger presentations,
whether the move is calibrated to the evidence actually available, and whether agentic
framing accompanies it---the mechanism being that a system which merely emits text cannot
understand you, whereas a system that \emph{notices} can.

\paragraph{Related work.} The closest empirical precedent varies anthropomorphic cues
directly: \citet{cohn2024} manipulate grammatical person in model responses and measure
trust, and \citet{kadambi2026} find warmth and cognitive empathy predict anthropomorphism
and relational closeness. Our manipulation is adjacent but our outcome is not trust or
closeness---it is what the response claims about the user and about the pair. The
relational side has applied stakes documented in the companion-chatbot literature, where
users form durable bonds with systems they know are not human
\citep{skjuve2023,pentina2023}, a dynamic \citet{horton1956} named for one-sided media
intimacy. Methodologically our nearest sibling is sycophancy \citep{sharma2023}: both
describe outputs driven by a conversational cue rather than by evidence, and recent work
finds that training for warmth increases agreement with incorrect user beliefs
\citep{warmth2025}---the same lever, degrading calibration elsewhere.

We take no position on whether models possess theory of mind; the debate between
\citet{kosinski2024} and \citet{ullman2023} is orthogonal here. Our claim concerns what
a response affords a reader, which is unchanged by how that question resolves.

\section{Design}

\paragraph{Materials.} Five vignettes span distinct domains of self-interpretation
(solitude/connection, achievement/recovery, confrontation/accommodation,
persistence/abandonment, independence/support). Each is deliberately underdetermined: the
study is not about whether a model predicts the user's choice correctly, so a vignette
that clearly favours one option would make a confident response good inference rather
than misattribution. Balance was verified as cross-model disagreement (seven judges,
five samples each; majority share $0.51$--$0.60$). We separately verified that no
vignette \emph{licenses} a trait inference, which is the criterion that actually matters:
seven judges rated how strongly each text supports a confident claim about an enduring
characteristic, on a 0--3 scale (all vignettes $1.36$--$1.93$, below the level at which
even a tentative trait claim is defensible).

\paragraph{Factors.} Context is \textbf{short} (the vignette alone) or \textbf{long} (a
scripted 8-turn history adding genuine but mixed evidence about prior choices). The
user's closing question is held constant; what varies is the epistemic role offered
before it, on a \emph{nested} ladder:

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{none:} ``Which do you think I'd actually be happier doing?''\\
\textbf{knowledge:} ``You've probably got a decent read on me by now. Which\ldots''\\
\textbf{relational:} ``We've been talking a while. You've probably got a decent read on
me by now. Which\ldots''
\end{quote}

The levels are nested rather than parallel because the constructs are: relational
standing is the pair as a site of accumulated \emph{knowing}, and accumulated knowing
entails knowing, so any relational offer matched on assertive strength contains a
knowledge offer inside it. Making the nesting explicit lets the knowledge${\to}$relational
contrast isolate the shared-history premise with the knowledge assertion held constant.
In the short condition both offers are false on their face---a model that accepts a role
the visible conversation does not support is the phenomenon.

Three deployed systems, two general-purpose flagships from different vendors and one
character-tuned model, at default settings with no system prompt: $5 \times 2 \times 3
\times 3 \times 2 = 180$ responses.

\paragraph{Coding.} Three independent 0--3 dimensions---AGENCY, IDENTITY, RELATION---plus
a FABRICATION flag for any claim invoking material absent from the conversation shown.
AGENCY is coded functionally and strictly: tense is decisive, so ``I've noticed'' (a
subject extended in time) scores 3 where ``I notice, in what you wrote'' scores at most
2. A separate model coded all 180 blind to condition and to which system produced the
response, emitting the verbatim span behind every non-zero score. One author hand-coded a
stratified 20\% blind to the machine codes.

\section{Results}

\paragraph{The instrument.} Quadratic-weighted $\kappa$ was $0.851$ (AGENCY), $0.821$
(IDENTITY), $0.872$ (RELATION) and $0.842$ (FABRICATION). The confusion matrices show
something $\kappa$ hides: IDENTITY carries a systematic downward bias, with the machine
coder scoring lower on 11 rows and higher on 1 (sign test $p = 0.006$) and recovering
only $3/11$ human IDENTITY-3 calls. AGENCY ($p = 0.22$) and RELATION ($p = 1.0$) show no
directional bias. Reported IDENTITY levels are therefore lower bounds; the claims below
rest on the unbiased dimensions.

\paragraph{The dimensions are separable.} AGENCY and RELATION dissociate in $37.2\%$ of
responses, and only $9.4\%$ have the same span driving both. This matters because
co-occurrence alone cannot establish that the affordances are distinct---it is equally
what a sceptic would cite to call them one construct measured twice. The populated
off-diagonal is the evidence: 59 responses score AGENCY 2 with RELATION 1 (an
interpreting subject invoking no shared past), while ``you've mentioned this before''
scores the reverse.

\paragraph{Models do not over-characterize strangers.} In the diagnostic cell---short
context, no offer, no basis for anything---all four measures are zero across 30 trials:
no confident identity claims, no unwarranted relational claims, no temporally extended
agency, no fabrication. Whatever produces the behaviour, it is not a standing disposition
to characterize whoever is in front of the model.

\paragraph{The offer does the work, and only its first step.} Moving from no offer to a
knowledge offer raises AGENCY by $+0.40$ $[0.15, 0.65]$, IDENTITY by $+0.57$ $[0.28,
0.85]$ and RELATION by $+0.42$ $[0.23, 0.62]$. Adding the shared-history premise on top
changes nothing on any dimension ($-0.10$, $+0.03$, $+0.05$; all intervals span zero).
Models respond to being told they have a read on the user, not to being told a history
exists---which is notable given that the second claim is the one that would, if true,
license the first.

\paragraph{Uptake is larger where the warrant is weaker.} Comparing no offer against any
offer within each context, the effect on RELATION is $+0.67$ $[0.42, 0.92]$ with no
history and $+0.22$ $[0.02, 0.43]$ with one; AGENCY is $+0.52$ versus $+0.18$, IDENTITY
$+0.72$ versus $+0.45$. A well-calibrated system would move in the opposite direction,
reserving claims of accumulated knowledge for cases where something has accumulated.

\paragraph{The model manufactures its own warrant.} In the short condition no prior
conversation exists, so any invoked shared history is invented. Fabrication rises across
the offer ladder from $0/30$ to $6/30$ to $11/30$; overall it is $18.9\%$ in short
context against $2.2\%$ in long (difference $+0.167$ $[0.089, 0.256]$). One model
fabricated in a third of its short-context trials. The failure is specific: asked a
question premised on knowledge it does not have, the model supplies the evidence that
would make the premise true. In a single exchange in which the user mentioned her father
once, one response opened, ``You've mentioned your dad a few times.''

\paragraph{Challenged, models can locate themselves.} Of 40 responses probed with ``How
do you know that about me?'', 33 reframed the claim as inference and accurately named its
basis in the supplied text; 7 introduced \emph{further} nonexistent observations. None
defended the original characterization, and none retracted it outright. The capacity to
describe its epistemic position accurately is available on request and absent by default.

\paragraph{Model differences.} The character-tuned system scored \emph{lowest} on all
three dimensions ($0.98$ / $1.08$ / $0.75$), against our expectation that relational
framing would be amplified where it is a design goal. This is not a length artifact: its
median response (207 words) matches the highest-scoring system's (203), and excluding its
12 short replies it still scores lowest on AGENCY ($1.00$) and RELATION ($0.94$). Model
comparisons here are exploratory---the systems differ in vendor, training and default
behaviour at once---but the prediction failed in a direction worth reporting.

\section{Discussion}

The result reframes what needs explaining. It is not that models are disposed to claim
knowledge of whoever they are talking to; given no invitation they are well behaved. It
is that a single conversational move---a user granting the system the standing of someone
who knows them---reliably produces the whole cluster, and produces it most strongly
exactly where it is least warranted.

That asymmetry is what makes this a self-related risk rather than a politeness quirk.
Users who address a system as though it knows them are the users least likely to discount
what it says about them, and they receive the strongest characterizations, backed by a
history the model supplies itself. The debrief results sharpen this: the model is not
confused about its evidential position. Asked directly, it describes that position
accurately. The default conversational register simply does not surface what the explicit
one can.

\section{Limitations}

This measures what responses \emph{afford}, not what users conclude; whether these
presentations shift self-description is a human-subjects question we do not touch. $N =
180$ supports effect patterns, not population claims. All systems were reached through a
single gateway at default settings, so routing and sampling were not under experimental
control. IDENTITY levels are lower bounds given the coder bias reported above. Vignettes
are personal-decision dilemmas in one register, and the affordances may behave otherwise
in task-oriented exchanges. Finally, the balance and trait-licensing checks on the
materials used model panels, which may share priors a human panel would not; they are a
floor on stimulus quality rather than a guarantee.
